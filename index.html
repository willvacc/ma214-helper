Chapter 8–9

Confidence Intervals & Hypothesis Tests

Confidence Interval — the big idea

You don't know the true population mean μ. You take a sample, compute x̄, and build a range around it. That range is the CI — a bet that the true μ is somewhere inside.

Structure: x̄ ± (critical value) × (standard error). Wider CI = less precise but more confident.

σ known vs unknown

σ known → use z (rare in practice)
σ unknown → use t with df = n−1 (almost always)

Key z values: 90% → 1.645 | 95% → 1.96 | 99% → 2.576

Hypothesis Testing — 6 steps

1

State hypotheses — H₀ (null) and Hₐ (alternative)

2

Set α — usually 0.05

3

Compute test statistic — how many SEs is x̄ from μ₀?

4

Find p-value — probability of seeing your data if H₀ were true

5

Decision — reject H₀ if p-value < α

6

Conclusion in plain English

Left-tailed

Hₐ: μ < μ₀
p-value = area to LEFT

Right-tailed

Hₐ: μ > μ₀
p-value = area to RIGHT

Two-tailed

Hₐ: μ ≠ μ₀
p-value = 2 × one-tail area

Type I Error (α): Reject H₀ when true. | Type II Error (β): Fail to reject when false.

Key Formulas

CI for μ — σ unknown

x̄ ± t(α/2, n−1) × (s / √n) df = n − 1

CI for proportion p

p̄ ± z(α/2) × √(p̄(1−p̄) / n) Requires: np̄ ≥ 5 AND n(1−p̄) ≥ 5

Test statistic for μ (σ unknown)

t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n) df = n − 1

Test statistic for proportion p

z = (p̄ − p₀) / √(p₀(1−p₀) / n)
Worked Example — One-Sample t-test

Problem

A company claims batteries last 300 hours. You test 16 batteries: x̄=292, s=20. Test at α=0.05.

1

H₀: μ=300 | Hₐ: μ≠300 (two-tailed)

2

α=0.05, two-tailed → α/2=0.025 each tail

3
t = (292−300)/(20/√16) = −8/5 = −1.60 df = 15
4

From t-table df=15, two-tailed: |t|=1.60 < t₀.₀₂₅,₁₅=2.131 → p-value > 0.05

5

Fail to Reject H₀

6

Insufficient evidence at 5% level to conclude batteries last different than 300 hours.

Chapter 10

Inference for Two Populations

10.1 — Independent Means t-test

Two separate groups. Run F-test first for equal variances. Then pooled or unequal variances t-test.

10.2 — Paired Samples t-test

Same subjects measured twice. Compute dᵢ=x₁ᵢ−x₂ᵢ, then one-sample t-test on d̄.

10.3 — Two Proportions z-test

Compare p₁ vs p₂. Use pooled p̄_c under H₀: p₁=p₂.

Key decision: Independent (10.1) or Paired (10.2)? If each obs in group 1 is linked to one in group 2 → Paired. Otherwise → Independent.

F-test for Equal Variances (runs BEFORE 10.1)

H₀: σ²₁=σ²₂ vs Hₐ: σ²₁≠σ²₂. F=s²₁/s²₂, df₁=n₁−1, df₂=n₂−1.

p < α → reject H₀ → use two-sample t-test assuming unequal variances
p ≥ α → fail to reject → use pooled t-test

Prof. Gorgievski: "two-sample t-test assuming unequal variances" — NOT "Welch's t-test."

Two-sample t-test — unequal variances

t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / √(s²₁/n₁ + s²₂/n₂) df (Satterthwaite) = (s²₁/n₁ + s²₂/n₂)² ───────────────────────────────── (s²₁/n₁)²/(n₁−1) + (s²₂/n₂)²/(n₂−1)

Paired t-test

dᵢ = x₁ᵢ − x₂ᵢ d̄ = mean of dᵢ's s_d = std dev of dᵢ's t = d̄ / (s_d / √n) df = n − 1

Two-proportion z-test

p̄_c = (x₁+x₂)/(n₁+n₂) (pooled under H₀) z = (p̄₁−p̄₂) / √(p̄_c(1−p̄_c)(1/n₁+1/n₂)) CI uses UNPOOLED: √(p̄₁(1−p̄₁)/n₁ + p̄₂(1−p̄₂)/n₂)
Worked Example — Paired t-test

Problem

5 employees before/after training. Before: 72,68,74,65,71 | After: 75,70,78,67,74. Test if training improved scores (α=0.05).

1

H₀: μ_d=0 | Hₐ: μ_d<0 (before−after, negative=improvement)

2

d = −3,−2,−4,−2,−3 → d̄=−2.8, s_d=0.837

3
t = −2.8/(0.837/√5) = −2.8/0.374 = −7.49 df = 4
4

t₀.₀₅,₄=−2.132. t=−7.49 < −2.132 → in rejection region → Reject H₀.

Chapter 11

Inference for Variance

11.1 — One variance χ² test

H₀: σ²=σ²₀ using chi-square. Always requires normality. Right-skewed, always positive. df=n−1.

11.2 — Two variances F-test

H₀: σ²₁=σ²₂. F=s²₁/s²₂. df₁=n₁−1, df₂=n₂−1. Two-tailed needs both critical values.

Chi-square two-tailed test needs TWO critical values: χ²_α/2 (upper) and χ²_(1−α/2) (lower).

CI for one variance

[ (n−1)s² / χ²_(α/2) , (n−1)s² / χ²_(1−α/2) ]

Chi-square test for one variance

χ²_(n−1) = (n−1)s² / σ²₀ df = n − 1 Right-tail (Hₐ: σ²>σ²₀): p = area RIGHT of χ² Left-tail (Hₐ: σ²<σ²₀): p = area LEFT of χ² Two-tail (Hₐ: σ²≠σ²₀): p = 2 × min(left, right)

F-test for two variances

F = s²₁ / s²₂ df₁=n₁−1, df₂=n₂−1 Upper critical: F_(α/2, df₁, df₂) Lower critical: 1 / F_(α/2, df₂, df₁) [flip the df!]
Worked Example — Chi-square test for variance

Problem

Analyst claims variance > 280(%²). n=10, s²=361. Test α=0.05.

1

H₀: σ²≤280 | Hₐ: σ²>280 (right-tailed)

2
χ² = (9)(361)/280 = 3249/280 = 11.60 df=9
3

p-value = 0.2366 > 0.05 → Fail to Reject H₀. Insufficient evidence to support analyst's claim.

Chapter 12

Chi-Square Tests

12.1 — Goodness-of-Fit

Do observed counts match a hypothesized distribution? df = k−1.

12.2 — Test for Independence

Are two categorical variables related? Uses contingency table. df = (rows−1)(cols−1).

Core formula (both tests)

χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E sum over all cells

Always right-tailed only. Large χ² = big O vs E differences = evidence against H₀.

Expected counts — independence test

E_ij = (Row i total × Column j total) / Grand total ALL expected counts must be ≥ 5

Degrees of freedom

Goodness-of-fit: df = k − 1 (k = categories) Independence test: df = (r−1)(c−1) (r=rows, c=cols)
Worked Example — Test of Independence

Problem

Under 35: UA=174, Nike=132, Adidas=90 | 35+: UA=54, Nike=72, Adidas=78. Independent? α=0.05.

1

H₀: Independent. Hₐ: Dependent. df=(2−1)(3−1)=2

2

Row totals: 396, 204. Col totals: 228, 204, 168. Grand=600.

3
E(Under35,UA)=396×228/600=150.48 E(Under35,Nike)=134.64 ...
4
χ² ≈ 22.15 χ²₀.₀₅,₂=5.991 22.15 > 5.991 → Reject H₀

Chapter 13 · Final Focus

Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)

Why not just run multiple t-tests?

3 groups pairwise at α=0.05 inflates overall error to ~14%. ANOVA tests all groups at once, keeps α=0.05 globally.

SST = SSA (between groups) + SSE (within/error). F=MSA/MSE. Large F = factor matters.

One-Way ANOVA (13.1–13.2)

1 factor. H₀: μ₁=μ₂=...=μₖ. After rejecting H₀, use Tukey's HSD to find which pairs differ.

Two-Way ANOVA (13.3–13.4)

2 factors. Tests row effects, column effects, and interaction — three F-tests from one ANOVA.

Randomized Block Design (13.3 — no interaction, 1 obs/cell)

Factor A = treatment. Factor B = block (nuisance variable). Block absorbs variation from SSE → smaller SSE → larger F for A → more power.

Two-Way WITH Interaction (13.4 — 2+ obs/cell)

Interaction: effect of A depends on level of B.

If interaction is significant → do NOT interpret main effects alone.

3 Assumptions

  • Independence
  • Normality
  • Equal variance

Chapters 14–15 · Final Focus

Simple & Multiple Regression

The model

Sample: ŷ=b₀+b₁x | Population: y=β₀+β₁x+ε

OLS minimizes SSE=Σ(yᵢ−ŷᵢ)². Residual = actual − predicted.

Key metrics

R²=SSR/SST — % variation in y explained. Range 0–1.

Adj R² — penalizes extra predictors. Use to compare models.

s_e=√MSE — avg residual size in original units.

Slope interpretation

"For every 1-unit increase in x, y increases/decreases by b₁ on average."

Multiple: "...holding all other variables constant."

Association, not causation.

Hypothesis test for slope

H₀: β₁=0 | Hₐ: β₁≠0 | t=b₁/s_b₁ with df=n−2 (simple) or n−k−1 (multiple)

PI vs CI

CI for mean y at x — narrower.
PI for individual y at x — always WIDER (adds individual variability).
Both widen as x moves from x̄.

Assumptions — LINE

  • Linearity
  • Independence of residuals
  • Normality of residuals
  • Equal variance (homoscedasticity)

OLS formulas

b₁ = Σ[(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ)] / Σ[(xᵢ−x̄)²] b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄

Sums of Squares

SST = Σ(yᵢ−ȳ)² total variation in y SSR = Σ(ŷᵢ−ȳ)² explained by model SSE = Σ(yᵢ−ŷᵢ)² unexplained/residual SST = SSR + SSE R² = SSR/SST = 1 − SSE/SST s_e = √(SSE/(n−2)) simple; use n−k−1 for multiple
Reading R Output — what to look for on the exam

Typical lm() output

Coefficients: Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|) (Intercept) 12.450 3.211 3.88 0.0015 ** x 2.370 0.480 4.94 0.0002 *** Residual standard error: 5.21 on 18 degrees of freedom Multiple R-squared: 0.575, Adjusted R-squared: 0.551 F-statistic: 24.4 on 1 and 18 DF, p-value: 0.0001

How to read it

b₀

Intercept=12.450. When x=0, predicted y=12.45. p=0.0015 → significant.

b₁

Slope=2.370. For every 1-unit increase in x, y increases 2.37 on average. p=0.0002 → highly significant.

R²

0.575 → 57.5% of variation in y explained. Adj R²=0.551 for model comparison.

df

18 df for error → n−2=18 → n=20. Back-calculate n from df on the exam.

Chapter 20 · Final Focus

Non-Parametric Statistics

What and when?

No distribution assumptions — uses ranks instead of raw values. Use when: clearly non-normal, small sample, or ordinal data.

Trade-off: more flexible, but less powerful. Always prefer parametric when assumptions are met.

Parametric Non-parametric equivalent Section
Two-sample t-test (independent) Wilcoxon Rank-Sum 20.1
Paired t-test Wilcoxon Signed-Rank 20.2
One-Way ANOVA Kruskal-Wallis 20.3

How ranking works

Combine all data, sort smallest to largest, assign ranks 1,2,3... Tied values get the average of the ranks they'd have occupied.

Wilcoxon Rank-Sum (20.1) 2 independent groups

H₀: Populations identical | Hₐ: Differ

1. Combine n₁+n₂ obs, rank ALL together 2. T = sum of ranks for smaller sample 3. Compare T to Wilcoxon table

Wilcoxon Signed-Rank (20.2) paired

H₀: Median difference=0

1. dᵢ = x₁ᵢ − x₂ᵢ 2. Drop zeros 3. Rank |dᵢ| 4. Assign + or − from sign of dᵢ 5. T⁺=sum(+ranks), T⁻=sum(−ranks) 6. T=min(T⁺,T⁻) → Wilcoxon table

Kruskal-Wallis (20.3) 3+ groups

Uses chi-square table (NOT Wilcoxon table).

H = [12/n(n+1)] × Σ(Rⱼ²/nⱼ) − 3(n+1) H ~ χ² df = k − 1 (k = number of groups)

Kruskal-Wallis is the ONLY non-parametric test here that uses the chi-square table.

Worked Example — Wilcoxon Rank-Sum

Problem

Group 1: 12,18,9,25 | Group 2: 21,7,30,15,11. Test if populations differ α=0.05.

1

H₀: Identical. Hₐ: Differ. n₁=4, n₂=5. Smaller=Group 1.

2
Sorted: 7,9,11,12,15,18,21,25,30 Ranks: 1,2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9
3

Group 1 ranks: 12→4, 18→6, 9→2, 25→8. T=4+6+2+8=20

4

Table (n₁=4,n₂=5,α=0.05 two-tailed): reject if T≤11 or T≥29. T=20 → Fail to Reject H₀.

Step 1 — Type of data?

Categorical counts? → Chi-square (Ch 12)
Numeric outcome? → Step 2

Step 2 — How many groups?

1 group: Mean → t-test (9). Variance → χ² (11).
2 groups: Step 3.
3+ groups: ANOVA (13) or Kruskal-Wallis (20).

Step 3 — Independent or Paired?

Independent: F-test first, then two-sample t-test (10.1)
Paired: Paired t-test (10.2)
Proportions: Two-prop z-test (10.3)

Step 4 — Normality violated?

Yes → switch to non-parametric equivalent (Ch 20).

Most common exam trap: independent vs paired. Most common second trap: which chi-square formula (GOF vs independence → different df).
